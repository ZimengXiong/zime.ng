    I can never seem to enjoy Hack Club
                                                                27 apr 2026


   If you're anyhow engaged in hobbyist tech circles, you've likely heard
   of Hack Club. They're a non profit that encourages teens to learn
   technical skills like coding and hardware engineering.

   They're a bit confusing to get into (dozens of events (e.g
   YouShipWeShip, FlavorTown, HQScraps), scattered across hundreds of Slack
   channels), but they mostly rely on one thing to hand out their credits,
   points, giveaways, and prizes: time spent on a project. They do this by
   having you install a time tracker. I don't like this idea a lot, and
   it's the main reason I haven't submitted any projects 'officially' to HC
   (I mainly enjoy talking to people about them instead), it's a classic
   example of Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to
   be a good measure.


   ## Time Spent ≠ Competence
   --------------------------

   Time Spent, for the same reason as LOC or commits, is not a good
   indicator of technical ability or quality of output and encourages bad
   engineering behavior.

   I understand that they likely don't have the resources to manually
   grade/rate each project, and that time spent (when tracked correctly) is
   one of the better indicators of effort put into a project, but measuring
   "time spent" equates activity with productivity. It is a fallacy that
   ignores the reality of how problems are solved.

   You might jump to conclusions and think I am referring to the cliche
   comparison between an experienced engineer who can solve a bug in ten
   minutes that takes a novice six hours, but for this case I actually do
   think time is a fair metric of actual effort put in, just at different
   skill levels.

   What is really problematic is the quality vs. volume tradeoff that this
   structure incentivizes. Programming should reward finding the most
   elegant, efficient path to a solution. That most elegant and efficient
   path often is not the most technically superior—a single user todo list
   doesn't (and shouldn't) need PostgreSQLs backed by Redis.

   Incentivizing students to hit time targets encourages them to
   overcomplicate things (and often not in a way the student finds fun,
   imagine finishing a project only to be 2 hours away from a prize, you
   would likely want to spend those two hours adding features you never
   wanted in the first place).


   ## A lot of work happens outside the IDE
   ----------------------------------------

   Participants are implicitly pressured to keep their editors open or
   their trackers running to maximize rewards, rather than focusing on the
   natural, often irregular, flow of creative work.

   This is the main reason I haven't submitted or 'tracked' any time. My
   workflow is too often sporadic and it happens everywhere. When I have an
   idea that I want to build...I just go build. The last thing I want to
   worry when I have five windows open is if my time tracker is installed
   and counting my current session(s); I just want to build.

   Their trackers also don't count time spent researching or just thinking,
   which I think is a more indicative measure of effort (for example, when
   building a core component of WriteAhead (soon^TM), I needed to
   accurately track the location of the text cursor across cocoa, swift,
   and electron apps; there wasn't an API on Earth, private or public, that
   could do that; it took well over tens of hours of research to puzzle
   together a robust heuristic to accomplish this. the actual time spent in
   the IDE? maybe one or two.).

   The only time I am reminded of Hack Club during a project is when I
   finish and think back about how many hours I could've logged for the
   project, only if I had just configured `hackatime`.


   ## HackClub knows it's a bad metric
   -----------------------------------

   To be fair to the team at HQ, they aren’t stupid. They know that time is
   a flawed proxy for value. After talking with some folks at HQ about why
   they use time as a metric, I started to realize that they are optimizing
   for something else.

   When the worst of LLMs can generate a functional webpage in six seconds,
   shipping a project is no longer the hurdle it used to be. The real
   challenge for a non-profit trying to "inspire a generation of engineers"
   is persistence. They need a proof of work mechanism that encourages a
   beginner to sit and struggle with a compiler error, and develop the
   muscle memory of building. For someone just starting, 40 hours of "time
   spent" is a life-changing milestone.

   Looking at the success of HackClub, whatever they've been doing, it
   clearly seems to be working, and maybe the tradeoff here is just right
   for engaging newcomers.
