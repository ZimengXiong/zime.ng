    Stop Romanticizing Open Source
                                                                23 apr 2026


   NOTE: This article was written in a VERY pre-Claude Code world, and may
   not reflect accurately now

   Somewhere along the way, we started to treat open source as such a moral
   stance. People will tell you to use GIMP instead of Photoshop "because
   it's free (both libres and gratis)," ignoring that GIMP has been a
   massive PITA to use for 20 years. They'll recommend Linux for your
   laptop because "Windows is spyware," then not give a fuck when you can't
   figure out why your printer isn't connecting and then blame you for your
   obscure software choices.

   This happens because we have turned our software choice into an
   identity. Picking Firefox over Chrome or LibreOffice over Word is now a
   form of virtue signaling that creates a cult around showing that you're
   ethically superior or simply more technically sophisticated. But
   software really is just a tool, and treating it like a lifestyle just
   leads to some very bad decisions.

   I've learnt a long time ago to just use what works. The reality is that
   funded software generally beats unfunded software, regardless of
   license. The indie, volunteer-driven open source that gets romanticized
   is rarely competitive, and the exceptions only prove why funding
   matters. While open source software is often praised for its
   transparency, flexibility, and community-driven innovation, these
   advantages do not always translate into better user
   experiences—especially for complex, consumer-facing tools.


   ## Corporate-backed open source is, for projects that matter, the only
   viable open source
   ----------------------------------------------------------------------

   Everyone loves to hate Chrome, but Chromium's Blink engine is
   objectively excellent, it's fast, standards-compliant with developer
   tools that crush the competition. It's open source, but it's fast
   because Google needs it to be (they bet billions on the future of work
   being on the web). It's fast because Google dedicates entire teams on
   it, not because of community volunteers.

   The open source software that actually powers the world works the same
   way. Linux dominates servers because IBM, Google, and Meta pay
   developers to work on it full-time. React is everywhere because Meta
   supports it. Kubernetes, Go, TensorFlow, Android, VS Code,
   TypeScript—all funded by tech giants with dedicated teams.

   This isn't "community" open source. This is "we open-sourced our
   internal tools" or "we fund this to commoditize our complements." The
   code is open, but the development still remains professional and
   salaried.


   ## The exceptions that prove the rule
   -------------------------------------

   "Wait," you say, "what about Blender? OBS Studio? VLC? (I won't get to
   VLC here, but VLC hasn't been great for years). They're volunteer
   projects with great UX!"

   Yes, they exist. But look closer at where they came from. Blender was
   built by professional commercial animators working at a company.

   Ton Roosendaal co-founded NeoGeo in 1988, a Dutch animation studio that
   became the largest 3D animation house in the Netherlands, creating
   award-winning work for Philips and other corporate clients. Within
   NeoGeo, Roosendaal was responsible for software development, and he
   started building Blender in 1994 as an in-house tool to solve real
   commercial production problems.

   When NeoGeo wound down, Roosendaal founded Not a Number (NaN) in 1998, a
   commercial company with a freemium business model that raised €4.5
   million in venture capital and employed 50 people at its peak. Blender
   only became open source in 2002 after NaN failed and the Blender
   Foundation raised €100,000 to buy the rights—with help from several
   ex-NaN employees who had already spent years developing it as commercial
   software.

   Blender is great despite being open source, not because of it. It
   carries the DNA of two commercial companies (NeoGeo and NaN) and only
   achieved its current polish after securing millions in grants from Epic
   Games, Apple, and AMD to hire full-time developers. You need money to
   beat the odds.

   This pattern repeats everywhere. The best open source tools are built by
   people who learned their craft at proprietary companies on salary. OBS
   is not only sponsored but also developed by Logitech, YouTube, Twitch,
   and AMD, who all have vested interests in OBS succeeding.


   ## There's nothing noble about struggling with bad software
   -----------------------------------------------------------

   Now look at what happens when open source is actually built by
   volunteers in their spare time. GIMP has been the "Photoshop
   alternative" for two decades and still has a UX that sends new users
   straight back to Adobe. LibreOffice has been hailed as the Word or Docs
   alternative, yet still refuses to parse .docx and other industry
   standard formats, however proprietary, accurately.

   The free software movement/FOSS community is one of many communities
   (e.g. 3D Printing, coffee) where some extremist members pride themselves
   with conviction on how hard and complex the things they use are to
   differentiate themselves from the "noobs" in a obnoxiously strange show
   of intellectual superiority through pity asking and ego stroking.

   But software should not be hard to use. Software should be used to drive
   productivity. Software that is hard to use is, simply put, bad software.


   ## Burnout
   ----------

   The worst part of the romanticization is how it treats the people
   building the software. Users adopt volunteer open source for their
   business, make money off it, then demand bug fixes. When the unpaid
   maintainer doesn't respond in 24 hours, they leave angry comments about
   "unprofessional" abandonment.

   You can't have it both ways. Either accept that free tools come with no
   guarantees, or pay for software that includes support. The result of
   this entitlement is burnout: 60% of open source maintainers have
   considered quitting because users treat them like employees while
   refusing to pay them.


   ## Just use what works
   ----------------------

   Sometimes open source is the right choice. If you're running a web
   server, you probably want Linux and nginx. If you're building an
   application, you probably want Postgres and Python. These are genuinely
   best-in-class tools that merely happen to be open source. Furthermore,
   if you are relying on single-purpose libraries, focused CLI tools, or
   one-off apps, the volunteer-driven FOSS model works flawlessly.
   Small-scoped tools built by developers for developers don't need
   corporate sponsors to succeed.

   But when it comes to massive, complex, user-facing applications, the
   rules change entirely. If you're a professional photographer using GIMP
   instead of Photoshop because of "principles," you're just costing
   yourself money. If you're forcing your company onto LibreOffice instead
   of Word or Docs, you are allowing your productivity to tank and support
   tickets to skyrocket for the sake of ideology.

   Software is a tool. Proprietary applications are tools that are
   sometimes better because money pays for development and polish and
   support. Open source programs are tools that are sometimes better
   because transparency, privacy, and community matter more for
   infrastructure. The license is irrelavent (for most people)—it doesn't
   make you a good person or a bad person for using it.

   Software is a utility, so pick the tool that actually does the job you
   need it to do.
